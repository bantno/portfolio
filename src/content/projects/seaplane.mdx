AHAB (Aquatic High-endurance Aerial Biomonitor) is a solar-powered seaplane UAV designed for persistent marine mammal monitoring over remote ocean regions. The project spans conceptual vehicle design with a novel wing-rotation mechanism, an MDP-based flight planning framework that outperforms heuristic approaches, and a phased prototype flight test campaign.

---

<section id="motivation">

## The Problem

Cetacean species — whales, dolphins, and porpoises — are experiencing sustained population declines worldwide, and marine biologists need better tools to study and protect them. Current monitoring platforms each have critical limitations: ship-based surveys are expensive and weather-dependent, fixed-wing UAVs like ScanEagle require manual launch and recovery with limited range, surface vehicles like SailDrone are too slow for focal-animal tracking, and passive acoustic buoys cannot be repositioned. A solar-powered seaplane fills this gap by combining persistent deployment through water landings and solar recharging, sufficient airspeed for focal monitoring, autonomous operation that eliminates ship-based recovery costs, and dual-modality sensing — visual survey while airborne, acoustic monitoring via hydrophone while floating.

</section>

<section id="conceptual-design">

## Conceptual Design

The seaplane's design integrates aerodynamic performance, hydrostatic stability, and solar energy harvesting into a single airframe. A **flying hull** configuration was selected over floatplane and amphibious alternatives for its reduced structural weight and better takeoff performance in rough seas. The design process iterates through geometry definition, mass estimation, parasitic drag analysis in OpenVSP (C_D₀ ≈ 0.031), endurance calculation, and hydrostatic stability checks — tuning beam, deadrise angle, and forebody/step/sternpost proportions — until all requirements are met.

Because the vehicle must rest and recharge on the open ocean, the hull cross-section is sized for roll stability while floating: placing the center of gravity above the center of buoyancy at the design waterline yields a positive metacentric height so the hull self-rights across the expected range of heel angles.

A custom spanwise **wing-rotation mechanism** lets the moored vehicle tilt its solar panels toward the sun during the recharge phase, and the same actuators and mechanical lock perform self-righting from an inverted attitude — no additional hardware. Over a simulated week in late June at 25°N, the articulated wing collects **+203 Wh** net over a fixed-wing baseline, a **21.9% energy gain** that widens the latitude and seasonal envelope over which the vehicle can close its daily energy budget. Solar modeling with pvlib and Ascent Solar CIGS thin-film parameters shows the vehicle can sustain 1.3 hours of flight per day in January and 2.3 hours in June at the target latitude — meeting the requirement of at least one flight-hour per day year-round.

## Optimal Flight Planning

A solar-powered seaplane must decide when to fly, when to rest on the water and recharge, and how to balance observation time against the risk of battery exhaustion or crash. Weather is stochastic — solar irradiance and wind speed vary unpredictably — so simple threshold rules ("fly when battery is above X, land when below Y") are intuitive but leave performance on the table.

I formulated this as a **Markov Decision Process** and solved it via stochastic dynamic programming (Bellman recursion). The state captures battery state-of-charge and operating mode (floating, flying, or failed); at each stage the optimal policy selects from takeoff, cruise, land, or save-energy based on current conditions. The simulation is driven by stochastic weather models fit to ERA5 reanalysis data — solar irradiance as a Beta-distributed clearness index and wind speed as a Weibull distribution — at five candidate deployment sites spanning 20°N to 58°N. The value function is computed offline and stored onboard for real-time decision-making.

The interactive demo below lets you design a threshold rule and race it against the optimal policy. The key insight: the MDP makes non-obvious decisions — like resting through a sunny period to bank energy for a risky overnight segment — that no fixed threshold can replicate.

Monte Carlo studies across battery capacities, failure penalties, geographic locations, and seasons show the optimal policy consistently outperforms every threshold parameter combination — more whale sightings and lower failure rates — and adapts automatically without the location-specific tuning a threshold controller needs. A practical design takeaway: the energy-optimal battery capacity is **mission-location dependent**, so battery sizing should follow the intended operating environment.

## Prototype: AHAB

AHAB is a proof-of-concept prototype built to demonstrate that the airframe can execute the missions the MDP framework plans. Development follows a phased flight test approach: aerodynamic validation, hydrostatic and hydrodynamic validation, subsystem integration, and full mission demonstration. The airframe is 3D-printed in LW-ASA, reinforced with carbon-fiber spars and fiberglass, and waterproofed with marine-grade sealant and electronics potting. Modifications from the conceptual design include a V-tail for easier construction and sponsons for improved hydrostatic stability.

<Figure src="/images/seaplane/Preflight.jpg" alt="AHAB prototype during pre-flight inspection" caption="AHAB during pre-flight inspection." number={1} wide={true} />

### Phase 1: Aerodynamic Validation

Phase 1 confirmed the airframe is flightworthy. AHAB flew with minimal trim corrections, significant excess thrust, and excess control authority in cruise. Measured performance: 3.75 kg all-up weight, ~150 W cruise power at 17 m/s and 35% throttle, a glide ratio of 6.5, and a 4–5 second takeoff run.

### Phase 2: Hydrostatic & Hydrodynamic Validation

Phase 2 validates water operations. The second prototype is fully waterproofed and hydrostatically stable. Waterproofing tests and low-speed taxi runs are complete; remaining evaluation includes hump speed, planing behavior, step ventilation, and water takeoff.

<Video src="/images/seaplane/Low Speed Taxi Side View.mov" caption="Low-speed water taxi test." number={3} />

### Future Phases

Phase 3 integrates the wing-rotation mechanism, self-righting controller, solar-recharge system, and hydrophone winch. Phase 4 executes a complete mission cycle — takeoff, survey, landing, recharge — to validate the full concept of operations.
