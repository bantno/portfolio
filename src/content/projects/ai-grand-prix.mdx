## The Competition

The [AI Grand Prix](https://www.theaigrandprix.com/) is an autonomous drone racing competition conceived by Anduril founder Palmer Luckey, run with the Drone Champions League and Neros Technologies — a **$500,000** prize pool and engineering roles at Anduril. Teams qualify in simulation, then fly the same autonomy on a real drone, with the top ten advancing to the championship.

## Phase One — The Simulator Qualifier

The organisers disable GPS, attitude, position — **and the gate coordinates.** Everything the drone knows about the course, it has to see and solve for in flight.

**Result: 7th of 3,000+ teams.** First clean full-course lap on 22 July 2026; best counted lap 17.56 s, all 17 gates, zero collisions, pure vision.

<Details summary="The flight stack" hint="perception, estimation, guidance, control">

Every subsystem runs onboard, in the flight loop, in real time.

- **Gate perception.** A real-time detector turns each camera frame into gate measurements, with an explicit confidence attached to every measurement so the estimator knows how far to trust it. It runs under a fixed per-frame compute budget, so a busy frame can never starve the control loop.
- **State estimation.** An error-state Kalman filter fuses the raw IMU with the camera's gate measurements to recover attitude, velocity and position from scratch. Measurement noise is calibrated from data rather than hand-tuned, and every correction is statistically gated. Position held to **~0.8 m RMS** against ground truth over a 253 m lap.
- **Guidance and control.** A geometric tracking controller flies a smooth reference through the gate openings, with a speed governor that trades lap time against cornering and descent limits. Successive tuning took the lap from a conservative 22 s to the 17.56 s record.
- **Verification.** Flight accuracy is scored only against an independent ground-truth observer, never the estimator's own state. Two test suites plus roughly 60 standalone tests check the filter's Jacobians numerically, its covariance health and its statistical consistency; a batch A/B harness ran tuning campaigns as a version-controlled experiment corpus alongside the code.

</Details>

## Phase Two — The Physical Qualifier

The same stack has been brought up on real racing quadrotors, one subsystem at a time, with a safety pilot holding the arm and override switches throughout.

<Video src="/images/ai-grand-prix/onboard_gate_detection.mp4" caption="The gate detector running on onboard video from the qualifier venue." />

Where it stands, as of late September 2026:

- **Fully autonomous hover, three flights for three.** Take off, hold a commanded height for 30 s and land, with the pilot touching nothing but the arm and override switches. Horizontal hold **0.15–0.19 m RMS** by the onboard estimate, with roughly **1,600 gate measurements fused per flight**.
- **Real-time onboard.** The vision and estimation path was ported to C++ and verified bit-identical against the Python reference on logged flights. The detector runs at **15 ms/frame median** on the drone's embedded GPU module, against 99 ms in Python, delivering **24–34 accepted gate fixes per second** in flight.
- **Course laps.** Autonomous gate passages are confirmed on video, and the longest autonomous flight to date entered both laps of the course, logging **17 gates and 195.6 m of path in 137.5 s** before the pilot took over. The full course has not yet been completed autonomously.

<Details summary="What changes on hardware" hint="none of the simulator's guarantees survive">

The simulator handed over clean, timestamped IMU samples, an ideal pinhole camera and a race-status feed naming the active gate. The real drone gives none of that: an IMU streamed from a commercial flight controller with no sensor timestamps, a rolling-shutter camera behind a real lens, no feed saying which gate is next, a course map converted from the organiser's drawing rather than an as-built survey, and the flight controller's own loops sitting between the control law and the motors. Control also has to pass from a human pilot's sticks to the autonomy in flight, and back on demand.

</Details>

<Details summary="Bring-up discipline" hint="why the order matters">

Nothing gets built on an unverified subsystem. Compute and the flight-controller link first; then camera calibration; then logged manual flights; then state estimation from the IMU alone, tuned on those logs; then the handover from pilot to autonomy; then vision on top of the IMU-only estimator; and only then guidance and control, flown as slowly as the law allows.

Two reasons for that order. **Isolation** — when the integrated system misbehaves, a verified subsystem can be ruled out instead of suspected. **Baselines** — each step leaves a measured number the next one is judged against. Three airframes each carry their own version-controlled calibration set, and every flight writes a self-describing onboard log that replays offline, so estimator tuning costs no flight time.

</Details>
